Cane Corso Crate Size by Age and Sex: 42, 48, 54 or 60 Inch?

Owner-reported fits, why the number on the box is not the space inside, and a puppy divider schedule that saves you from buying three crates.

Home›Blog›Cane Corso Crate Size

Published: 2026-10-19 · Updated: 2026-10-19 · Cane Corso World

Picking the right Cane Corso crate size is the one gear decision new owners most often get wrong twice: first a crate that fits the 10-week-old puppy, then a "large" crate that the 9-month-old already lies in with his nose against the bars. Nobody wants to be the owner posting a photo of a 110 lb guardian folded into a box like a deck chair, so this guide gives you the sizes owners actually report, the measurements that matter, and a divider plan for the months in between.

Quick answer: most adult females fit a 48-inch crate, and most adult males need a 54-inch crate. Buy a 60-inch (or a giant-breed kennel) only for a tall, long male or a dog over roughly 130 lb. A 42-inch crate is a puppy-only size. Buy the adult size once and use a divider to shrink the space while your puppy grows.

What size crate does an adult Cane Corso need?

A 48-inch crate suits most adult females. A 54-inch crate suits most adult males. Plan for 60 inches only if your male is tall, long-bodied or well above the standard weight.

The breed standard gives the range you are planning for. According to the FCI standard as summarized on the Wikipedia Cane Corso entry, males stand 62–70 cm (24–28 in) at the withers and weigh 45–50 kg (100–110 lb), while females stand 58–66 cm (23–26 in) and weigh 40–45 kg (90–100 lb). Withers height is the shoulder, not the head, and a standing Corso carries its head well above the shoulder. Crate height therefore has to clear the head, not the back.

What owners actually report

In an r/CaneCorso thread on crate size for a future full-grown female, one owner wrote that their 90 lb adult female "fits into a 48inch kennel fine." Another owner bought a 48-inch collapsible crate for a 12-week-old male and said a 40-inch "probably would have worked" but was not worth the risk once he is full grown. In a newer thread on kennel and crate size, the advice was more blunt: "Get the biggest you can." One owner keeps both 54- and 48-inch crates. Another bought "the biggest one possible and still not big enough." A third warned that a 48-inch crate was fine for a puppy, but they sell a 56-inch one too, and you will need the bigger one sooner rather than later. Their male weighs 135 lb.

That spread is the whole problem. "Biggest possible" from a big-box store often means 48 inches, which is a comfortable female size and a tight male size. If you have a male, do not trust the word "XL" on the box. Trust the inches.

Why is the crate size on the box not the space inside?

The number on the box is usually the crate's outside length. The space your dog lies in is shorter and lower because of the frame, door hardware and, on plastic or rotomolded kennels, the wall thickness.

The gap is small on wire crates and bigger on solid kennels. RuffLand publishes both figures for its largest model, the Titan kennel. On the outside it measures 48 in long, 33.25 in wide and 34.75 in high. On the inside it is 46.125 in long, 29.25 in wide and 33.5 in high. That is almost 2 inches of length and 4 inches of width lost to the walls. RuffLand also says its sizing results are suggestions and that final size selection is the owner's responsibility. That is a polite way of saying "measure your dog."

Height is the hidden failure

Owners argue about length, but height is what makes a big Corso look cramped. A crate that is long enough but lower than your dog's standing head height forces the dog to stand with its head down. PetSmart, for example, lists a 48" L x 33" H size for its Top Paw double-door crate. A tall male standing 28 inches at the shoulder can need more headroom than that, which is one reason owners of big males jump straight to 54 inches, where the crates are taller as well as longer.

How do you measure a Cane Corso for a crate?

Measure nose to base of tail with the dog standing, then floor to top of head with the dog standing naturally. Add about 4 inches to the length and 3 inches to the height. Those are your minimum interior dimensions.

  1. Pick a calm moment. Measure after a walk, using a soft tape and a treat to hold a stand.
  2. Length: run the tape from the tip of the nose to where the tail meets the body. Do not include the tail, which tucks or curls when lying down.
  3. Height: measure floor to top of head (or ear tips, whichever is higher) while the dog stands naturally, not stretching up for a treat.
  4. Add margins: +4 in length so the dog can stretch out, +3 in height so the head is not touching the roof.
  5. Compare with interior figures: look for the interior length on the spec sheet. If only exterior is listed, subtract about 2 in for a wire crate and more for a plastic kennel, or email the maker.
  6. Check the room: a 54-inch crate needs a wall with space for the door to swing. Measure your doorway too if the crate does not fold.

Humane World for Animals puts the basic standard simply in its crate training guide: the crate "should be large enough for your dog to stand up and turn around in," and if your dog is still growing, you should choose a crate size that will fit their adult size. For a Corso, "stand up and turn around" is the floor, not the goal. A giant breed also needs to lie flat on its side with its legs out.

Should you buy a 42-inch crate for a Cane Corso puppy?

Usually no. A 42-inch crate fits a Corso puppy for a few months at most. A divider in the adult crate does the same job and you only pay once.

The appeal of the 42 is real: it is cheaper, lighter and fits beside the bed. The trouble is that by six months most Corso puppies weigh 60 to 80 lb, and the 42-inch crate becomes a second purchase within one season. If you want a bedroom crate for the first weeks and a living-room crate later, that is a sensible two-crate plan. Just make the second one the adult size, not a 48 for a male "to see how big he gets."

When a smaller second crate does make sense

  • Car travel: a vehicle may only take a 42-inch or a narrow kennel, so the car crate and the house crate can differ.
  • Upstairs nights: a light folding crate in the bedroom for the first weeks of night-time potty trips.
  • Borrowing: Humane World for Animals notes that some local shelters rent crates, so you can trade up as your puppy grows instead of buying the in-between size.

Puppy divider schedule: when to move the panel

Set the divider so the space is the puppy's body length plus about 4 inches. Move it when the gap in front of the nose drops below 2 inches, or when the puppy can no longer lie flat without touching both ends.

The reason for the divider is housetraining. A puppy given the whole 54-inch crate often sleeps in one end and uses the other as a toilet. A puppy given just enough room to lie down tends to hold it. That only works if you also stick to realistic time limits. Humane World for Animals says puppies under six months should not stay crated for more than three or four hours at a time, and adult dogs not more than four to five hours during the day.

If your puppy soils the crate after a move

Slide the divider back one step for a week, shorten crate stretches, and go out straight after waking. Do not punish accidents. That teaches the puppy to hide them, not to hold on.

Wire crate, plastic kennel or heavy-duty crate?

Wire crates are the cheapest way to get 48 or 54 inches with a divider. Rotomolded kennels lose interior space to their walls but travel well. Heavy-duty steel crates are for dogs that bend or escape from wire.

Checklist before you buy a Cane Corso crate

  • Sex and expected adult build noted, using the breed-standard ranges above.
  • Dog measured: nose to tail base, and standing head height.
  • Interior length at least body length + 4 in; interior height at least head height + 3 in.
  • Divider confirmed to fit that exact crate model (fit varies between models of the same length).
  • Floor space and door swing measured where the crate will live.
  • Bed or mat sized to the interior, not the label, so it lies flat without bunching.
  • A re-measure reminder set in your phone for every two weeks until six months.
  • A daily schedule that keeps crate stretches within the time limits above.

Common Cane Corso crate size mistakes

Buying "the biggest one at the store"

Big-box stores often top out at 48 inches. That is a female size. For a male, look for 54 inches before you need it, not after he has outgrown the 48.

Reading the outside measurement

As the RuffLand figures show, a 48-inch kennel can be about 46 inches inside. Two inches matters when your dog is already at the edge.

Ignoring headroom

A dog that has to stand with its head lowered will avoid standing, shift around and treat the crate as a trap. Check the height, not just the length.

Leaving the divider in too long

The divider is a housetraining tool, not a permanent setting. A teenage Corso still squeezed into puppy space is easy to spot: legs folded, back pressed to the panel. Owners who want a calm adult dog give that space back on schedule.

Using the crate to replace exercise

Even the right crate is not a way to skip walks or training. A crated Corso still needs daily exercise and contact. Also think about the rest of your routine.

Does crate size matter for joints and long-term comfort?

Crate size will not prevent or cause joint disease, but a crate your dog can lie flat in, on a padded floor, is more comfortable for a heavy giant breed.

Corsos spend a lot of hours lying down, and a big dog on bare wire or plastic is lying on hard ground. Put a firm, flat bed in the crate, sized to the interior. If your dog has had hip screening or a joint issue, ask your vet what bedding they would suggest. If you rent, keep a note of the crate and its location too.

Frequently asked questions about Cane Corso crate size

Is a 48-inch crate big enough for a Cane Corso?

For most adult females, yes. One owner reports their 90 lb female fits a 48-inch crate fine. For most adult males, 48 inches is tight, and a 54-inch crate is the safer buy.

What size crate for a male Cane Corso?

Plan on 54 inches for a typical male in the 100–110 lb standard range. Tall, long or heavier males, especially those over about 130 lb, may need a 60-inch crate or a giant-breed kennel. Measure first.

What size crate for a Cane Corso puppy?

Buy the adult size and use a divider. Set the usable space to body length plus about 4 inches, and move the divider every two weeks while the puppy is under six months.

Is a 42-inch crate ever right for a Cane Corso?

Only as a short-term puppy or travel crate. By six months most Corsos weigh 60 to 80 lb, and a 42-inch crate becomes too short.

How long can a Cane Corso puppy stay in a crate?

Humane World for Animals advises no more than three or four hours at a time for puppies under six months, and no more than four to five hours during the day for adult dogs.

Should I measure inside or outside dimensions?

Inside. Product names usually give the outside length. RuffLand's Titan kennel, for example, is 48 inches outside but about 46 inches inside.

The bottom line on Cane Corso crate size

Do not be the owner who buys a 42, then a 48, then a 54. For most homes, the right Cane Corso crate size is 48 inches for a female and 54 inches for a male, chosen by interior length and headroom rather than the label. Fit a divider for the puppy months and move it on a schedule. Measure once a month after six months, and the crate will fit your dog at every age instead of shaming you in the next photo.
